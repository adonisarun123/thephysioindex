Stairs are one of the first places knee trouble shows up. Many people in India live in homes with stairs and no lift, climb to a first-floor clinic or office, or squat and sit cross-legged through the day, so a knee that complains on the stairs quickly gets in the way of daily life. The good news is that most stair-related knee pain is common, not dangerous, and responds well to the right exercise and a few practical changes.

This guide explains why stairs are hard on the knee, the usual causes, what you can do now, and when to see a physiotherapist or a doctor.

## Why do stairs hurt when walking on flat ground feels fine?

Climbing and descending stairs asks your knee to bend under your full body weight. Going up, the thigh muscles (quadriceps) push you up. Going down, the same muscles work as brakes, controlling your weight while the knee bends. Both movements press the back of the kneecap against the thigh bone far harder than walking on level ground.

That is why many people notice pain **going down** more than going up. If the joint behind the kneecap is irritated, or the muscles around the hip and knee are not yet strong enough for the load, stairs are where it shows first.

## What are the common causes?

A physiotherapist will examine you before naming a cause, but these are the patterns seen most often.

### Pain at the front of the knee (patellofemoral pain)

A dull ache around or behind the kneecap that is worse on stairs, squatting, kneeling or after sitting for a long time with the knee bent (for example in a car, cinema or office). It is common in teenagers, runners, and adults who have recently become more active. There is often no single injury.

### Knee osteoarthritis

More common after the age of 45. Pain can be felt all around the knee or in one area and is often worse on stairs, getting up from a chair or the floor, and after long walks. Stiffness first thing in the morning usually eases within about half an hour. Osteoarthritis is not simply "wear and tear": staying active and strengthening the muscles is one of the main treatments.

### Tendon pain below the kneecap

Pain just below the kneecap, often sharp when going down stairs or after jumping sports. It tends to build gradually with a sudden increase in activity.

### A twisting injury (meniscus or ligament)

Pain on the inner or outer side of the knee after a twist, sometimes with swelling, catching or the knee "giving way". These need an assessment rather than waiting it out.

## What can I do now?

These are general steps that are safe for most people. They do not replace an assessment.

- **Use the handrail** and take one step at a time while the knee is sore.
- **Lead with the stronger leg going up, and the sore leg going down.** A simple way to remember it: "up with the good, down with the sore".
- **Do not stop moving altogether.** Rest for a day or two after a flare-up, then return gradually. Knees generally do better with regular, sensible activity than with long rest.
- **Break up long sitting.** Stand and straighten the knees every 30 to 45 minutes.
- **Ease off what clearly makes it worse** for a few weeks: deep squats, kneeling, sitting on the floor or on low stools, and long walks down steep slopes.
- **Footwear matters.** Cushioned, supportive shoes are usually more comfortable than flat chappals for long periods on your feet.
- **Weight and general fitness.** If you are carrying extra weight, even a modest reduction lowers the load on your knees on every step.

Avoid starting a strenuous exercise programme from videos while the knee is painful. The right exercises, at the right level, depend on the cause, and doing too much too soon is a common reason pain lingers.

## How does a physiotherapist help?

At the first appointment, a physiotherapist asks how the pain started and what makes it better or worse, then watches you move: walking, a step-up and step-down, a squat, and how your hip and ankle work with the knee. They check strength, flexibility and the knee itself, and look for signs that need a doctor or a scan.

Treatment for the common causes above is usually:

- **A graded strengthening plan** for the thigh and hip muscles, starting at a level you can manage without a big flare-up and progressing over weeks.
- **Activity changes** that keep you moving while the knee settles, including how you manage stairs at home and at work.
- **Short-term pain relief strategies** where helpful, such as taping or advice on ice or heat.
- **A clear timeline**, so you know what improvement to expect and when to review.

Most people need a few sessions spread over several weeks, with exercises done at home in between. Strength takes time to build, so the plan matters more than any one session.

Physiotherapy can be at a clinic or, if travelling is hard, at home. A home visit is often practical for older adults or anyone who cannot manage the stairs to a clinic.

## When should I get help?

**Get urgent medical help** if:

- you cannot put any weight on the leg after a fall or injury,
- the knee looks deformed or the kneecap has moved out of place,
- the knee is hot, red and swollen and you feel unwell or have a fever,
- the knee suddenly locks and will not straighten.

**Book an assessment** with a physiotherapist or doctor if:

- the pain has lasted more than two to three weeks and is not improving,
- the knee swells, catches or gives way,
- stairs, sleep or work are becoming difficult,
- you are unsure whether to keep exercising or rest.
