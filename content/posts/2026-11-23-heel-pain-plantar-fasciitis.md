If your heel hurts sharply with the first few steps out of bed, eases as you move around, and then aches again after a long day on your feet, the most common cause is plantar fasciitis, also called plantar heel pain. It is very common, it is rarely serious, and most people improve with simple changes to footwear, activity and daily stretching. A physiotherapist can confirm what is going on, rule out other causes and give you a plan that fits your day, whether you teach, run a shop, cook for a family or walk every morning.

This guide explains what is happening in the foot, what you can change at home, how physiotherapy helps and when to see a doctor.

## What is plantar fasciitis?

Under the sole of your foot is a thick, strong band of tissue called the plantar fascia. It runs from the heel bone to the base of the toes and supports the arch every time you stand, walk or run.

When this tissue is overloaded, the area where it attaches to the heel becomes irritated and painful. You will also hear it called plantar heel pain, which is the term many physiotherapy guides now use.

Typical signs are:

- pain on the bottom of the heel or along the arch,
- pain that is worst with the first steps after sleeping or sitting for a while,
- pain that settles a little once you get moving, then returns after long periods of standing or walking,
- pain when the sole is stretched, for example climbing stairs or lifting the toes.

## Who is more likely to get heel pain?

NHS and physiotherapy guidance list several factors that make plantar heel pain more likely:

- being aged roughly 40 to 60,
- spending long hours standing or walking, or a sudden increase in walking or running,
- starting a new exercise habit on hard surfaces,
- tight calf muscles or a stiff ankle,
- shoes with little cushioning or arch support,
- carrying extra body weight,
- diabetes or some inflammatory conditions.

Many everyday Indian habits fit this list. Walking barefoot at home on tiled or marble floors, wearing thin flat chappals or worn-out sports shoes, standing for hours in a kitchen, a classroom or behind a shop counter, or suddenly starting long morning walks in a new year's resolution can all add load to the heel.

## What can I do at home?

These are general measures that suit most people. They do not replace an assessment.

- **Change what is under your feet.** Choose shoes with a cushioned heel and good arch support, and keep a supportive pair of slippers for home. Avoid walking barefoot on hard floors, and limit flat chappals, flip-flops, backless slippers and high heels while your heel is sore. Off-the-shelf arch-supporting insoles or heel cushions help some people.
- **Reduce the load for a while, without stopping completely.** Cut back on long walks, running and standing where you can. Sit to chop vegetables or iron clothes. Swimming or cycling can keep you active with less pounding on the heel.
- **Stretch gently every day.** Regular, gentle stretching of the calf and the sole of the foot is part of most treatment plans. Ask a physiotherapist to show you which stretches suit you and how to tell if you are overdoing it.
- **Use cold for comfort.** A cold pack wrapped in a thin cloth can ease pain after a long day. Do not use cold if the feeling in your foot is reduced, as can happen with diabetes.
- **Look at your weight.** If you carry extra weight, even a modest loss reduces the load on your heels.
- **Ask about pain relief.** A doctor or pharmacist can advise what is safe for you, especially if you have other health conditions or take regular medicines.

Give these changes a fair trial. NHS advice is to see a doctor if heel pain has not improved after about two weeks of looking after it yourself.

## How does a physiotherapist help with heel pain?

At the first visit, a physiotherapist asks when the pain started, what makes it better or worse, your work and footwear, and your general health, including diabetes. They examine the foot, ankle and calf, watch how you stand and walk, and check for signs that point to a different cause, such as a nerve problem, a stress fracture or an inflammatory condition.

A best practice guide published in the British Journal of Sports Medicine, summarised by the UK's National Institute for Health and Care Research, recommends a core package for the first few weeks:

- **education** about the condition, footwear and how to manage your load,
- **daily stretching** of the calf and sole,
- **taping** of the foot to offload the painful tissue.

If that does not help after several weeks, the same guide describes a stepped approach, with options such as shockwave therapy, customised insoles and, later, referral to a doctor to discuss injections. Your physiotherapist will explain where you are in that path and refer you on if needed. As the pain settles, the plan usually moves on to strengthening the calf and foot and returning gradually to walking, sport or standing work.

Progress is usually measured in weeks to months rather than days. What you do between sessions, especially footwear and daily stretching, matters as much as the sessions themselves.

Physiotherapy for heel pain is usually done at a clinic. If walking or travel is difficult, some physiotherapists offer home visits.

## When should I get help?

**Get urgent medical help (call 112 or go to the nearest emergency department)** if:

- you hurt your foot in a fall or accident and cannot put weight on it, or it looks deformed,
- the heel or foot is hot, red and swollen and you have a fever or feel unwell,
- you have diabetes and notice a wound, blister, colour change or swelling on the foot along with pain or fever.

**See a doctor or physiotherapist soon** if:

- heel pain is severe or stops you working or doing normal activities,
- it is not improving after two weeks of self-care, or keeps coming back,
- you have tingling, numbness or burning in the foot,
- you have diabetes and any new foot pain,
- the pain is there at night or at rest, not just when you stand and walk,
- both heels hurt along with pain or stiffness in other joints or the lower back.
