A frozen shoulder is a painful, stiff shoulder that gradually loses movement, often with no injury to explain it. It is most common between about 40 and 60, in women, and in people with diabetes. It does get better on its own, but slowly, often over a year and a half to two years. Physiotherapy cannot switch it off, but it helps you control the pain, keep as much movement as possible, and carry on with work, sleep and daily life while it recovers.

This guide explains what is happening in the shoulder, what you can do now, what physiotherapy involves, and when to get help quickly.

## What is a frozen shoulder?

The shoulder joint sits inside a flexible bag of tissue called the capsule. In a frozen shoulder (doctors also call it adhesive capsulitis), this capsule becomes inflamed, then thickens and tightens. As it tightens, the ball of the joint has less room to move.

That is why a frozen shoulder feels different from a pulled muscle. It is not just painful to lift the arm; the shoulder physically will not go as far, even when someone else tries to move it for you.

## Who is more likely to get it?

Frozen shoulder can happen to anyone, but it is seen more often in:

- people aged roughly 40 to 60,
- women,
- people with diabetes,
- people whose arm has been kept still for a long time, for example after a fracture, surgery or a stroke.

With diabetes so common in Indian households, many people first hear about frozen shoulder from a relative who had one. If you have diabetes and notice your shoulder getting stiff, tell your doctor or physiotherapist, and keep up with your regular diabetes check-ups.

## What does it feel like, and why does it take so long?

Frozen shoulder usually develops in a pattern, although not everyone goes through each part clearly:

- **The painful phase.** The shoulder aches, often badly at night, and lying on that side is hard. Movement gradually becomes more limited.
- **The stiff phase.** The pain often eases, but the shoulder stays stiff. Reaching behind your back, combing or plaiting hair, tucking in a shirt, fastening a blouse or putting on a seat belt becomes difficult.
- **The recovery phase.** Movement slowly returns over months.

NHS information describes recovery taking at least 18 months to two years for many people, and sometimes longer. That long timeline is normal for this condition. It does not mean treatment has failed.

## What can I do now?

These are general steps that suit most people. They do not replace an assessment.

- **Keep the arm moving gently.** Long periods of keeping it still usually make stiffness worse. Use the arm for light everyday tasks within comfort.
- **Do not force it.** Hanging from a bar, pulling the arm hard with a towel or getting someone to "crack" it free tends to inflame the shoulder and can set you back. Some discomfort with gentle stretching is expected; sharp or lasting pain afterwards is a sign you have done too much.
- **Try heat for comfort.** A warm (not hot) pack or hot water bag, wrapped in a cloth, often eases the ache. Check your skin, especially if you have diabetes and reduced sensation.
- **Help your sleep.** Lie on your back or the other side with a pillow supporting the painful arm.
- **Adapt daily tasks for now.** Front-opening clothes, keeping frequently used things on lower shelves, and using the other hand for heavy bags all reduce the strain.
- **Ask about pain relief.** Your doctor or pharmacist can advise what is safe for you, particularly if you have diabetes, stomach problems, asthma or take other medicines.

Avoid copying stretching routines from social media videos without advice. The right exercises depend on how irritable your shoulder is at that moment, and that changes over the months.

## How does a physiotherapist help?

At the first visit, a physiotherapist asks how the problem started, what you can and cannot do, and about your general health, including diabetes and any history of injury. They then measure how far the shoulder moves on its own and with help, check the neck and upper back, and look for signs that point to something other than a frozen shoulder.

Treatment is matched to the stage you are in:

- **When pain is the main problem,** the focus is on calming it: gentle movement within comfort, sleep and posture advice, and ways to keep doing daily tasks.
- **When stiffness is the main problem,** stretching becomes progressively firmer, and the physiotherapist may use hands-on techniques to help the joint move.
- **Throughout,** you get a home plan, because what you do daily between sessions matters more than any single session.

Physiotherapy for a frozen shoulder is usually spread over several weeks, with reviews to adjust the plan. Your physiotherapist will also tell you if your progress suggests seeing a doctor about other options, such as an injection or a specialist opinion.

Physiotherapy can be at a clinic or, for older adults and people who find travel hard, at home.

## When should I get help?

**Get emergency help (call 112 or go to the nearest emergency department)** if:

- shoulder or arm pain, especially on the left side, comes with chest pain or tightness, breathlessness, sweating or feeling faint,
- you have fallen and the shoulder looks deformed or you cannot move the arm at all,
- the shoulder is hot, red and swollen and you have a fever or feel unwell.

**See a doctor or physiotherapist soon** if:

- shoulder pain and stiffness are not improving after a few weeks,
- pain is stopping you sleeping, working or looking after yourself,
- you have a history of cancer, or you have unexplained weight loss, new cough or breathlessness along with the shoulder pain,
- you have diabetes and your shoulder is getting stiffer.
